- **Zero-Budget: Constraint as Design Challenge** | Week 1 Kickoff
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Decided to embrace zero-budget constraint as portfolio narrative strength. Chose free tools (Figma Free, Webflow Starter, Notion) and framed it as "real-world problem-solving" rather than waiting for funding or asking Neo to invest.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    This felt like the only realistic option—neither Neo nor I have budget for premium tools. But also saw opportunity: most real projects have budget constraints, so this demonstrates resourcefulness. Hiring managers should value "delivered with $0" over "had unlimited budget."
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - Constraints breed creativity—Webflow Starter's 2-page limit forced elegant IA solution
    - Narrative framing matters: same situation = "limitation" or "strength" depending on how I tell it
    - Free tools are powerful enough for professional work—skill matters more than budget
    - Every constraint accepted = cascade of downstream decisions (entire project architecture flows from this)
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    This decision defines the entire project's identity. Portfolio story is literally "I built a professional site with zero dollars"—that's either impressive resourcefulness or it looks amateur depending on execution quality. The pressure is on to deliver professional results that prove tools don't matter, skills do.

- **Tech Stack Pivot: Webflow → Antigravity Hand-Coding** | Week 1-3 Initial, Week 4 Pivot
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Week 1-3: Selected Webflow based on Google Coursera recommendation + front-end showcase opportunity (custom code injection). Week 4: Discovered CRITICAL censorship blocker + market trend analysis triggered strategic pivot to Antigravity hand-coding.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    **Week 1-3 (Webflow)**: "Google recommends it" + "I can inject custom code to showcase front-end skills" + "Fast, secure hosting included." Felt solid.
    
    **Week 4 (Pivot trigger)**: Realized Webflow would **censor Neo's explicit artwork** under content policy—showstopper. Simultaneously researched market: designers increasingly expected to deliver functional prototypes + production code. "Vibe coding" = emerging standard, not niche skill. Hand-coding with Antigravity = deeper learning + censorship-proof + market positioning.
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - **Content policy due diligence = critical**: Should have vetted Webflow's content restrictions BEFORE committing. Censorship risk for client = non-negotiable blocker.
    - **Market trends inform tool choices**: Industry shifting toward designer-developer hybrid roles. Cursor aligns with where market is going, not just where it is.
    - **Sunk cost ≠ bad decision**: Week 1-3 Webflow learning wasn't wasted—understanding no-code platforms = valuable context for knowing when to hand-code instead.
    - **Multi-criteria tool evaluation**: Initial choice weighted "authority recommendation" too heavily. Pivot used: technical control + client risk + skill depth + market positioning.
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    - **Pre-project tool vetting checklist**: Before committing, check: (1) Content policy for client's specific needs, (2) Export/hosting autonomy, (3) Learning ROI vs. market trends, (4) Technical control boundaries.
    - **Earlier market research**: Should have surveyed job postings + design Twitter for "what tools are hiring managers asking for in 2026?" Week 1, not Week 4.
    - **Prototype content policy test**: Upload sample of Neo's explicit work to Webflow test project Week 1 to validate no censorship issues.
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    **The honest growth arc**: Week 1 = Trust authority (Google). Week 4 = Independent multi-criteria evaluation. This is the exact transition from junior → mid-level thinking.
    
    **Censorship blocker = portfolio gold**: Shows I prioritize client needs over personal convenience. Real-world constraint (content policy) → strategic pivot (autonomous hosting). Hiring managers want to see: "When blockers emerge, how do you adapt?"
    
    **Market positioning insight**: Antigravity pivot isn't just technical—it's career strategy. "Designer who codes with AI" = 2026 competitive advantage. Recognizing this = thinking beyond current project to long-term positioning.
    
    **Switching cost acceptance**: Pivoting Week 4 = short-term pain (re-learning) for long-term gain (skill depth + client protection + market alignment). Senior designers make these trade-offs consciously.

- **Google Methodology: Framework as Safety Net** | Week 1 Kickoff
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Committed to following Google UX Design Certificate methodology exactly (Design Thinking + Product Lifecycle). Every phase, every deliverable, by the book. Justified by belief in "their forma mentis and processes" plus portfolio/certificate completion needs.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    Two honest drivers: (1) I genuinely believe Google's approach is solid and industry-standard, (2) I need this to count as my certificate project AND produce portfolio artifacts. Following a proven framework feels safer than freestyling my first real project. If it goes wrong, at least I can say "I followed best practices."
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - Rigorous process produces documentation gold—every phase = portfolio artifacts
    - Framework structure prevents analysis paralysis—clear next steps when uncertain
    - "By the book" approach builds credibility with stakeholders (Neo trusts the process)
    - Methodology adherence = double-edged sword (thoroughness vs. potential over-documentation)
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    The honest question: Am I learning to THINK like a designer or EXECUTE a playbook? There's a difference. Junior designers follow frameworks religiously. Senior designers internalize principles and know when to adapt. Right now I'm firmly in "junior" mode—and that's okay for project 1. But I need to watch for the transition point where I start questioning the framework, not just obeying it.

- **Discord Research: Convenience Sampling Trade-offs** | Week 1 - Empathize
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Recruited 7 interview participants exclusively from Neo's Discord community. Rationale: zero budget (can't pay), these ARE the target users (fans, event attendees), Neo's endorsement increases participation rate.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    This felt pragmatic—Discord users are real fans, engaged, accessible, and free. Recognized potential bias (already fans = positive skew) but mitigated through interview protocol design: explicitly asked about frustrations with current platforms (YouTube, Instagram, Etsy), not just "what do you love about Neo?"
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - Convenience sampling ≠ representative sampling—this is existing fans, not new user acquisition insight
    - Interview protocol design can partially mitigate bias (asking for pain points worked)
    - 7 interviews yielded 3 distinct personas—clustering revealed real behavioral patterns, not just demographics
    - Authenticity matters: these users care about Neo, gave honest feedback because they want him to succeed
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    Should have recruited 2-3 "control" participants: people who follow similar artists but DON'T know Neo yet. Would provide contrast—what attracts vs. retains fans? Also missed guerrilla research opportunity: observe behavior on competitor sites (Behance, ArtStation) to see real browsing patterns vs. self-reported preferences.
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    The site design will optimize for retention (serving existing fans) not acquisition (converting new visitors). That might be the right strategy for Neo's current stage, but it's a blind spot I didn't consciously choose—it just happened because of sampling convenience. Need to explicitly acknowledge this limitation in case study: "Research focused on existing community; acquisition insights would require broader sampling."

- **Sabrina as Primary Persona: Emotional vs. Functional Design** | Week 2 - Define
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Prioritized Sabrina (Immersive Seeker—emotional connection, contemplative browsing) as primary persona for MVP. Marco (study tool) and Elena (professional bookings) marked as secondary, addressed in post-MVP phases.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    Research data showed Sabrina represents the largest user segment (casual fans/collectors) AND her needs drive brand differentiation. Atmosphere and contemplation = competitive advantage vs. transactional platforms like Etsy. Also, honestly, designing for emotional experience is more interesting to me than building search filters and booking forms.
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - Primary persona choice defines ENTIRE UX direction—every subsequent decision traces back to this
    - Emotional needs vs. functional needs: I chose emotional (soundtrack, hover states) over functional (advanced search, filters)
    - Phased approach allows serving secondary personas later without ignoring them completely
    - User segmentation creates clarity—can't serve everyone equally in MVP
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    This decision reveals my design values: I prioritize emotional experience over functional efficiency. That's not wrong—it aligns with Neo's brand—but it's a pattern I need to recognize. In future projects, would I still make this choice if the client's brand was "professional efficiency" instead of "raw artistic expression"? Or am I gravitating toward projects that let me do atmospheric design because that's what excites ME?
    
- **Started Documenting Sync Meetings Late** | 24 Jan 2026 | Week 3 - Ideate
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Didn't systematically document sync meetings until Sync 2 (19 Jan). Sync 1 (12 Jan) has no detailed notes, no transcript, no structured action items—just fragmented memory and bullet points.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    Honestly? Week 1 tunnel vision on "doing the work" and not looking amateur. Documentation felt secondary. Case study aspect wasn't even on my radar during that first sync.
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - **Case study documentation IS the work**, not separate from it
    - Documentation systems must be set up *before* project starts, not reactively
    - First conversations hold critical context that can't be fully reconstructed later
    - Student mindset ("do assignment") ≠ Professional mindset ("document process")
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    - **Pre-project setup phase**: Documentation infrastructure ready before kickoff (meeting templates, decision log, asset org)
    - **Explicit consent in kickoff**: "Can I record/transcribe our meetings for case study documentation?"
    - **Day 1 portfolio mindset**: Treat every interaction as case study material from first contact
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    This gap reveals I'm still thinking like a student, not a professional. Real designers bake documentation, handoff, and knowledge transfer into project DNA from inception. If I want to be taken seriously as a T-shaped designer, this muscle needs development *now*.

- **Layered Color System for Process Navigation** | 24 Jan 2026 | Week 3 - Ideate
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Designed a layered color-coding system for the project hub navigation table to create visual hierarchy and "card stack" effect. Used column colors (gray, purple, brown) as base layers plus strategic cell coloring to create depth perception in a flat table.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    "This table has 18+ deliverables across 4 dimensions—it needs to be instantly scannable." Wanted to apply Material Design depth principles but within Notion's constraints. Realized I could hack visual layering through color precedence.
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - **Constraints breed creativity**: Notion's limitation became the design opportunity
    - **Visual metaphors work**: "Card stack" metaphor made abstract process phases tangible
    - **Color as information architecture**: Strategic color use = instant navigation without reading
    - **Systems thinking in practice**: Understanding tool hierarchy (cell > row > column) unlocked new possibilities
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    - **Document visual systems earlier**: This pattern is reusable across projects
    - **Test in both modes**: Always check dark + light mode for color systems
    - **Build a pattern library**: Keep successful Notion design patterns documented
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    This is **design systems thinking** applied to project management tools. Shows I can create elegant solutions within constraints—critical skill for real-world design work where custom CSS or dev resources aren't always available. Portfolio gold.

- **HMW Scope Decisions Reveal My Prioritization Patterns** | 25 Jan 2026 | Week 3 - Ideate
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    While documenting HMW responses, realized I made critical scope decisions on the fly during sync without structured evaluation framework. Marked several features as "in scope" (soundtrack, hover interactions, tone-based form) that could technically derail MVP timeline given Webflow Starter constraints.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    "These immersive features ARE the differentiator, can't postpone them." Felt strong conviction that atmosphere features (Sabrina's HMW) = core value prop, not nice-to-have. But also aware I was making gut calls without validating technical feasibility first.
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - **Pattern recognition**: I consistently prioritize user emotional needs over technical safety—risky but aligned with UX-first thinking
    - **Constraint awareness gap**: Said "in scope" before confirming Webflow Starter can handle audio player + custom cursors
    - **Decision framework missing**: Need structured evaluation (Impact vs. Effort matrix) before committing scope
    - **Good instinct, sloppy execution**: Right to preserve differentiators, wrong to skip validation step
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    - **Immediately after HMW session**: Run technical feasibility spike for "in scope" features (1-2 hour Webflow test)
    - **Use Impact/Effort matrix**: Plot all HMW responses before calling scope decisions
    - **Define fallback options**: For every risky feature, document simplified alternative (e.g., "soundtrack" fallback = "ambient visual transitions only")
    - **Timebox validation**: "Tentatively in scope pending 2-hour tech validation by [date]"
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    This is the **exact mistake junior designers make**: championing great ideas without de-risking them first. In a real job, this creates sprint chaos when devs hit blockers week 2.
    
    **Growth opportunity**: I have strong UX instincts (prioritizing atmosphere = good call), but need to marry that with **risk management discipline**. Senior designers protect their good ideas by validating feasibility early, not discovering blockers mid-build.
    
    **Portfolio angle**: Documenting this teaches me to tell the story: "Initially scoped X, ran feasibility test, discovered Y constraint, adapted to Z solution." Shows strategic thinking, not just ideation.

- **The "Tone-Based Form" Idea: Brilliant or Alienating?** | 25 Jan 2026 | Week 3 - Ideate
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Proposed commission form with three tone options: "Insulti / Leccate / Richieste" (Insults / Flattery / Requests) during HMW session. Neo's raw, provocative brand inspired the idea. Marked as "in scope" but haven't validated if this actually serves users or just serves my desire to do something unconventional.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    "This is *perfect* brand expression—the form itself becomes an anti-establishment statement. Filters audience to people who 'get' Neo." Excited by how weird/unique it is. Felt clever.
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - **Risk I'm solving for myself, not users**: Unconventional ≠ user-centered by default
    - **Unvalidated assumption**: That tone-based segmentation helps vs. confuses/alienates potential buyers
    - **Missing user perspective**: Haven't tested if Elena (Event Seeker) or publishers would find this charming or unprofessional
    - **Brand alignment is real**: Neo's anti-establishment positioning DOES need expression in UX, question is HOW
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    - **Validate with Neo first**: "Does this tone match how you want to interact with commission clients?"
    - **Test with actual users**: Show mockup to 2-3 people matching personas, watch their reaction
    - **Define success criteria**: What would prove this works? (e.g., "Users laugh and engage" vs. "Users hesitate and leave")
    - **Build fallback version**: Standard form ready if testing shows tone options confuse/alienate
    - **A/B test plan**: If we ship it, track conversion rates vs. standard form baseline
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    This is my **ego talking**. I want to do something "different" for my portfolio, and that desire is clouding user-centered judgment.
    
    **The honest tension**: I think this idea *could* be brilliant brand differentiation, but I'm attached to it for the wrong reasons (it makes ME look creative). Real UX requires killing your darlings if users don't validate them.
    
    **What good looks like**: Present to Neo with enthusiasm + skepticism: "Here's a wild idea that could be perfect or totally backfire—let's test it before committing." Senior designers pitch bold ideas with built-in validation plans, not blind conviction.

- **Mobile-First Wireframing: Trusting the Process Over Coverage Anxiety** | 20 Jan 2026 | Week 3 - Ideate (Pre-Wireframing)
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    Planning paper wireframing session, felt pressure to document both mobile AND desktop versions to have "complete" deliverables. Realized I only had 2-3 hours and ~15 wireframes max. Made strategic choice: all mobile variants, zero desktop duplicates. Desktop happens later in Figma.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    Honest anxiety: "What if Neo asks to see desktop and I don't have it?" Felt incomplete not documenting both. But also recognized this was coverage anxiety, not strategic thinking. Mobile-first isn't just a buzzword—60%+ traffic IS mobile. Plus, showing Neo 3 homepage alternatives (mobile) is more valuable than 1 homepage mobile + 1 homepage desktop (same concept, different canvas).
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - **Coverage anxiety vs. strategic depth:** Junior mistake = trying to document everything. Senior move = document what matters most, deeply.
    - **Tool-appropriate work:** Paper = fast exploration. Figma = precise layouts. Don't force precision work into exploration tool.
    - **Client validation logic:** Better to validate DIRECTION (which homepage concept?) before BREAKPOINTS (how does it scale to desktop?)
    - **Mobile-first means mobile-FIRST:** Solve hardest constraint (thumb zones, 375px width) before easier one (desktop space)
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    Nothing—this was the right call. Caught myself falling into "complete documentation" trap and course-corrected before wasting time on desktop duplicates that add zero validation value.
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    **This reveals a maturity shift.** Old Leo would've stressed about "incomplete" deliverables and forced desktop wireframes to feel thorough. Current Leo recognizes that thoroughness ≠ value.
    
    **Strategic prioritization:** I'm learning to ask "What decision does this artifact enable?" not "What does complete documentation look like?"
    
    **The meta-lesson:** Design process isn't about checking boxes ("wireframes: mobile ✓ desktop ✓"). It's about sequencing decisions efficiently. Validate mobile UX direction with Neo → THEN adapt to desktop in Figma with precise grids.
    
    **Portfolio angle:** This shows I understand tool-appropriate work and strategic sequencing. Junior designers treat every deliverable as final documentation. Senior designers use deliverables as decision-making tools and choose the right fidelity for each decision.

- **Unified Form: Wireframing Reveals Over-Engineering** | 26 Jan 2026 | Week 3 - Ideate (Paper Wireframing)
    
    
    **What Happened**
    
    While paper wireframing the purchase flow, realized the "dual-mode contact form" (adapts based on cart state) was unnecessarily complex. Changed spec mid-wireframing: unified form for all scenarios (purchase, commission, general inquiry). Cart contents simply auto-included in email if present.
    
    **What I Was Thinking**
    
    Drawing the flow on paper made the complexity visible: "Wait, why do users need to see different form layouts? This is confusing." Original logic was "tailored experience = better UX" but wireframing revealed the opposite—mode-switching creates cognitive load without adding value. Unified form = simpler for users AND simpler to build.
    
    **What I Learned**
    
    - **Paper wireframing = bullshit detector**: Drawing flows reveals over-engineering that sounds good in abstract planning
    - **Simplification during ideation is GOOD**: Caught this before Webflow build, not after
    - **User mental models matter**: People understand "contact form," not "cart-mode vs inquiry-mode"
    - **Technical simplicity = UX simplicity**: Removing conditional logic benefits everyone (users, developer, client maintenance)
    
    **What I'd Do Differently**
    
    Nothing—this is exactly how it should work. Caught complexity early through low-fidelity prototyping. That's the whole point of wireframing.
    
    **Why This Matters**
    
    **This is a textbook example of why wireframing exists.** I could have built the dual-mode form in Webflow, spent hours on conditional logic, then realized during usability testing that users were confused.
    
    **Instead**: Caught it with pen and paper in 10 minutes. Zero wasted dev time.
    
    **Portfolio gold**: This moment demonstrates **iterative refinement** and **strategic simplification**—both core UX skills. Shows I'm not attached to my first ideas; I test and adapt.
    
    **Bigger lesson**: The best design decisions often involve *removing* features, not adding them. Unified form is stronger precisely because it does less mode-switching.
